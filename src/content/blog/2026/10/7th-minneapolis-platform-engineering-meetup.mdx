On October 28, 2026, I attended my first
[Platform Engineering Meetup](https://www.meetup.com/platform-engineering-minneapolis/)
hosted at Surly Brewing Co and put on by [Tarmac](https://www.tarmac.io/). It was the
first meetup that I have attended in a number of years, so I went in a little skittish and
wasn't sure what to expect. Hosted at Surly Brewing Co. in their upstairs private meeting
space, they had the bar open and a few snacks for attendees. Since I haven't been to a
meetup in a while, I figured I would go, keep my head down, and see where the evening took
me. A few people were already mingling, but I didn't want to interrupt them. I decided to
grab a drink, find an empty table, and finish reading an article from Anthropic about how
the [Claude Code](https://claude.dev/) team runs their
[pull request routine](https://claude.dev/blog/solving-code-review-at-anthropic/). Little
did I know how much that article would play into the rest of the meetup.

The first presentation was by
[Mike Osbone](https://www.linkedin.com/in/michael-osborne-cs/) from Blank Metal AI, "A Day
in the Life of an Agentic Engineer". In the presentation, Mike walked everyone through how
he weighed risk and human time while using AI to accomplish a task and get it out to
production. The slides progressed through the steps you could take after telling an agent
to implement a ticket from the ticketing system until it was merged. The ultimate goal was
to reduce the risk of what the AI agent produced while also lowering human involvement.
Ironically, this was exactly what I was reading from the Claude Code team earlier that
night. With the sheer number and size of pull requests, it's become difficult for humans
to decide whether the code is good. Yes, there may still be some AI "slop" in the
codebase, but depending on the change, looking through hundreds of files and hundreds of
lines of code can take humans hours, while it can also take AI minutes or seconds. Mike
came to the same conclusion in his slides.

He introduced us to James Reason's
[Swiss Cheese Model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_cheese_model). The Swiss Cheese
Model framework explains how accidents happen in complex systems because of layers of
failure. If you can identify all the different failure points and work to mitigate those
risks, you can lower your exposure to accidents. You may never reach 0 risk, but by adding
different layers, you can get close. So, in Mike's example, after telling the Orchestrator
to work through a ticket, a worker would do the work, pass it off to a fresh model to
review it, the next Copilot within GitHub would review it a second time, and a security
scan would run. Once both passed, a PR explainer would walk through the code and provide
screenshots. Finally, once those four steps are complete, a human-in-the-loop would review
it, with teammates and the person who opened the PR looking it over. Once everything is
validated, the code would be merged. This is something I've been considering for myself
and my work: how I can minimize my risk when using Claude Code and other AI Orchestrators
to do my work for me, and how I can spend less time trying to understand what they
produce.

The second presenter of the night was
[Hayden Goldstien](https://www.linkedin.com/in/hbgoldy/) from Greenhouse Software,
presenting "Building the Agentic Harness Your Business Actually Needs". In this
presentation, Hayden briefly described how Greenhouse created its own internal coding
agent called Spider Plant. With many companies talking about creating software factories
to replace developers and downsize their orgs, essentially, Greenhouse took a different
approach when they built out Spider Plant. The goal wasn't to be a software factory, but a
maintenance factory. As a maintenance factory, its job is to do all the work developers
dislike. That includes paying attention to Dependabot Alerts, fixing CVE issues, checking
observability alerts, and acting on them.

By building a maintenance factory that automatically handles those areas via MCP tools, it
frees developers to work on new features instead of maintaining what's already been
released. Hayden shared a slide showing how many PRs that spider plant opened in October
2026, and it was amazing to see how much work it did and how much it cost since its
inception. I don't remember the exact date, but it was only around $35 vs. the unknown
number it might have cost if a developer(s) were doing it themselves. The ultimate goal
for this maintenance factory is to, hopefully, at some point, do enough work to hire five
new developers, I believe he said, with the time/cost savings the factory is providing.

Overall, I'm really happy that I got back out there and attended last night's meetup. It
was great to meet new people, see what people are building at their companies, and expand
my knowledge. The idea of building a maintenance factory instead of a software factory
sounds interesting, and combining that with what Mike discussed—minimizing human
interaction to release those maintenance issues — sounds like a fun problem to solve; I'm
really looking forward to the next one.
